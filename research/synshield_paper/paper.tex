\begin{abstract}
Packet-to-image deep learning reports excellent results on DDoS benchmarks, but
how much of that performance survives evaluation on an attack the model has
never observed? SYNShield, a SYN-flood detector that encodes 0.3\,s traffic windows from CICDDoS2019 and classifies them with a ResNeXt-50 (32$\times$4d) network trained from scratch in Huawei MindSpore, is evaluated at three increasing levels of separation between training and test data. At
level~1, a randomly shuffled window split, DDoS F1 is 0.885 with perfect
recall. At level~2, held-out whole captures from the attack day used for
training, DDoS F1 is 0.866. At level~3, an entirely held-out attack day, DDoS
F1 collapses to 0.419. Levels~2 and~3 come from the same checkpoint, encoding and labels, differing
only in whether the test captures come from the training day, so that
comparison is controlled: cross-episode separation has a large effect
\emph{even after} capture-level blocking. The similar level-1 and level-2
scores suggest a smaller effect from window-level blocking but do not isolate
it, as those levels also differ in encoding. An independent audit against CICFlowMeter's official per-flow
labels confirms a 4\% label-boundary error rate among flagged windows and
raises DDoS F1 only to 0.453, indicating that known labeling errors explain a
small portion of the gap. Packet-image DDoS work should report a cross-episode result alongside any
same-episode one; the pipeline and all three protocols are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

TCP SYN flood attacks exhaust server connection-state resources by sending
large volumes of SYN segments without completing the three-way handshake. They
remain a substantial share of volumetric DDoS activity, and signature-based
detectors continue to struggle with rate-varied
variants~\cite{sharafaldin2019ddos}.

The \emph{packet-to-image} paradigm is an attractive response: raw packet bytes
are laid out as a two-dimensional pixel matrix and handed to a convolutional
network, which learns discriminative structure without manual feature
engineering~\cite{lotfollahi2020deep,bazzi2024resnet,shapira2019flowpic}.
Reported results on CICDDoS2019 are strikingly good, commonly 97--99\%
accuracy. The system presented here reproduces them, and that is where this
paper starts rather than where it ends.

Network captures are continuous and highly autocorrelated. Consecutive windows
share flows, endpoints and the same attack burst, so any evaluation whose test
set is drawn from the same capture session as the training set scores the model
on an episode it has already partially observed. That is an easier task than
detecting an attack never seen, which is what deployment demands. Related concerns about temporal and spatial
experimental bias are documented for security machine learning more
broadly~\cite{arp2022dos,pendlebury2019tesseract}. What has not been measured,
to the authors' knowledge, is the size of the effect for packet-image DDoS
detection, or \emph{which} form of separation is the one that matters.

To quantify the effect of train/test separation, the detector is evaluated at
three increasing levels. The model, encoding, and labels are held fixed across
the two levels supporting the central comparison. Moving from a randomly
shuffled window split to held-out captures from the same attack episode changes
DDoS F1 only slightly, from 0.885 to 0.866, whereas evaluation on a completely
held-out attack episode reduces DDoS F1 to 0.419.

These two comparisons carry very different evidential weight, and separating
them is central to what is claimed here. The second is controlled: one trained
checkpoint, one encoding, one set of labels, evaluated on two held-out sets
differing in exactly one respect, whether their captures come from the training
day. It shows that cross-episode separation matters a great
deal \emph{even once capture-level blocking is in place}, so blocking by
capture, the remedy usually prescribed for temporal leakage, does not by itself
establish that a model generalizes. The first comparison is suggestive only:
those two levels also differ in encoding, so it is reported as bounding the
effect of window-level blocking rather than measuring it.

Once a large gap is found, the obvious next question is whether it is real or
an artifact of the labeling used here. This is tested directly against
independent ground truth, and unsupervised detection is then examined as a
possible way around the problem. It does not, for a reason specific to this
representation.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Building on security-ML analyses of sampling and temporal
        bias~\cite{arp2022dos,pendlebury2019tesseract}, a controlled cross-episode measurement for packet-image SYN-flood detection is provided: from
        one checkpoint, DDoS F1 falls from 0.866 on held-out same-day captures
        to 0.419 on a held-out attack day, with encoding, labels and model held
        fixed (Section~\ref{sec:ladder}).
  \item To separate generalization failure from label quality in
        CICDDoS2019~\cite{sharafaldin2019ddos}, the held-out-day predictions are audited against CICFlowMeter's official per-flow labels, evidence independent of both the derived labels and the model's predictions. Of 2\,071 flagged windows, 86 (4\%) are confirmed
        boundary mislabels and none are confirmed benign; correcting them moves
        DDoS F1 from 0.419 to 0.453 (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
  \item Motivated by unsupervised intrusion detection~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}
        and deep one-class learning~\cite{ruff2018deep}, reconstruction- and distance-based alternatives are tested and a
        representation-specific failure mode is identified: SYN-flood windows
        are 3.7$\times$ \emph{sparser} than benign windows, producing an
        unexpected anomaly-score ordering in which attack samples are not
        assigned higher scores (Section~\ref{sec:unsup}).
  \item A reproducible ResNeXt-50~\cite{xie2017aggregated} / MindSpore~\cite{mindspore} pipeline is released, using established byte-image~\cite{bazzi2024resnet} and FlowPic~\cite{shapira2019flowpic} representations across all three train/test separation levels.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:related} places this among prior work and fixes the vocabulary
used for separation. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the system and defines
the three levels precisely. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results in the
order the investigation actually proceeded. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws
out the implications, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Feature-Based and Deep DDoS Detection} Early detectors used rule-based thresholds on statistics such as SYN/ACK ratio
or packet rate: fast and interpretable, but brittle against rate-varied
attacks. Machine learning on engineered flow features improves adaptability at
the cost of feature engineering and drift sensitivity. Deep models have since
been applied across intrusion detection, including autoencoder anomaly
scoring~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}; a recent survey reviews the full DL-IDS
pipeline and its open challenges~\cite{xu2025dlids}. A systematic review of 89
public NIDS datasets documents persistent quality problems and sets out best
practices for dataset use~\cite{goldschmidt2025datasets}, several bearing
directly on how train/test splits are formed.

\subsection{Packet-to-Image Representations} Lotfollahi et
al.~\cite{lotfollahi2020deep} showed that raw byte sequences fed to CNNs
classify encrypted traffic without decryption. Shapira and
Shavitt~\cite{shapira2019flowpic} proposed FlowPic, encoding a time window as a
packet-size $\times$ arrival-time histogram rather than a byte dump. Bazzi et
al.~\cite{bazzi2024resnet} applied the byte-dump idea to SYN flood detection on
CICDDoS2019 with ResNet-50, reporting 97.5\% accuracy.
ResNeXt-50~\cite{xie2017aggregated} and both encodings are adopted as
implementations; the contribution made here is the evaluation analysis, not the
backbone.

\subsection{Experimental Bias in Security ML} Arp et al.~\cite{arp2022dos} catalogue recurring pitfalls in security machine
learning, including sampling bias and temporal snooping, and argue that they
routinely inflate reported performance. TESSERACT~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}
shows this concretely for malware classification: enforcing temporal and
spatial constraints substantially lowers apparent performance. Closest to the present study, Cantone et
al.~\cite{cantone2024crossdataset} measure this at \emph{dataset} granularity:
classifiers near perfect within one dataset fall to roughly chance across
datasets. The present work asks the analogous question one level finer, within
a single dataset, where the unit of correlation is a continuous capture
belonging to a particular attack execution. That matters, because a
practitioner who avoids cross-dataset transfer entirely may still be reporting
same-episode numbers.

\subsection{A Vocabulary for Separation} \label{sec:vocab} Discussing this
precisely requires naming the levels at which a traffic dataset can be divided.
Three are used throughout, from weakest to strongest:

\emph{Window level} assigns individual time windows to splits
independently, so adjacent windows from one capture may land on opposite sides.
\emph{Capture level} assigns whole source captures as units, so no window in
test shares a capture with any window in train, though captures may still come
from the same attack execution. \emph{Episode level} assigns whole attack
executions as units, so the test set contains an attack the model has never
observed in any form.

\section{System Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Because the central E2\,$\to$\,E3 comparison concerns evaluation design, the
model, encoding, labeling and training configuration are held fixed across
those two conditions. This section describes the system and then defines the
three separation levels; E1 additionally uses the byte-dump encoding and is
therefore treated only as a contextual comparison.

\subsection{Threat Model and Problem Statement} A network tap observes traffic toward a protected service while an adversary
mounts a TCP SYN flood from distributed sources. The detector sees only packet
headers and payload bytes as captured: no flow-exporter features, no controller
telemetry, no endpoint instrumentation. Given a fixed window $W$, the task is
binary, and the target is $W = 0.3$\,s with deployment on commodity CPU.

\subsection{Dataset and Window Labeling}
CICDDoS2019~\cite{sharafaldin2019ddos} is used, which provides raw
PCAP archives with SYN-flood scenarios captured under controlled conditions.
Two capture days contain SYN attacks: \texttt{03-11-2018} (8 sub-captures, 1567
attack windows) and \texttt{01-12-2018} (3 sub-captures, 966 attack windows).
That the dataset contains exactly two attack executions is what makes the cross-episode experiment possible at all, and also bounds it, a point returned to in Section~\ref{sec:limits}. Limitations of this kind are common across public NIDS datasets~\cite{goldschmidt2025datasets}.

Packets are bucketed into non-overlapping windows of $W = 0.3$\,s indexed by
$b = \lfloor t/W \rfloor$ on the UTC packet timestamp. A window is labeled DDoS
if and only if \emph{both} conditions hold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the published CICDDoS2019 attack schedule overlaps the window
        (\texttt{03-11-2018} 11:28--17:35; \texttt{01-12-2018} 13:29--13:34,
        America/Halifax); \emph{and}
  \item the window contains more than 100 TCP SYN packets.
\end{enumerate}

The conjunction matters: the schedule alone marks an attack period, not which
windows within it carry attack traffic, while the SYN count alone would make
the label a deterministic function of a feature trivially visible to the
classifier. Requiring both grounds the label in published ground truth while
excluding idle windows inside the attack period. A residual risk
remains that the schedule's boundaries are themselves wrong;
Section~\ref{sec:audit} tests exactly that against independent labels.

\subsection{Encodings} \label{sec:encodings} Two window encodings are
implemented.

\textbf{Byte dump.} All packets in window $b$ are concatenated, zero-padded to
the next perfect square, reshaped into a square array and resampled to
$224\times224$ grayscale, following~\cite{bazzi2024resnet}. The transformation
is \emph{identical for both classes}: no class-conditional filtering is applied,
so the classifier cannot succeed by detecting a preprocessing artifact.
Figure~\ref{fig:samples} shows representative windows; flood windows exhibit
regular periodic banding produced by near-identical 64-byte SYN segments
repeating at high rate, whereas benign windows show irregular texture from
heterogeneous packet sizes and payloads.

\textbf{FlowPic.} Following~\cite{shapira2019flowpic}, a window becomes a
$64\times64\times3$ histogram over packet size (rows) and arrival time within
the window (columns), with channels for all packets, TCP SYN packets, and TCP
ACK/FIN/RST packets. Bin counts are $\log(1+x)$-scaled against a \emph{fixed
global} clip rather than per-image min--max, so absolute volume remains a
visual signal instead of being normalized away.

\subsection{Model and Training} The classifier is ResNeXt-50
(32$\times$4d)~\cite{xie2017aggregated}, instantiated through
\emph{mindcv}~\cite{mindcv} with a two-way head. ResNeXt replaces standard
convolutions with grouped convolutions of cardinality $C=32$ and per-group
width $d=4$, increasing representational diversity at comparable parameter
count.

The network is trained \emph{from scratch}: packet images share no low-level
statistics with natural photographs. Inputs are replicated across three
channels to match the backbone's expected shape; the byte-dump encoding uses
ImageNet mean/std constants inherited from the natural-image recipe and not
motivated by the data, while FlowPic runs use dataset-computed statistics.
Table~\ref{tab:hyper} lists the configuration, which is identical across every
run reported in this paper.

All splits are class-imbalanced toward benign traffic. This is addressed with
focal loss plus an $8\times$ minority-class weight boost, and checkpoints are
selected on validation DDoS-class F1 rather than accuracy or macro F1, so that
model selection tracks the operationally critical quantity.

\subsection{Three Levels of Train/Test Separation} \label{sec:protocols} The
three separation levels are now defined, instantiating the vocabulary of
Section~\ref{sec:vocab}. The
controlled E2\,$\to$\,E3 comparison varies only attack-episode separation,
while E1 additionally differs in encoding and is reported for context.

\textbf{E1: window level, random.} All window images are pooled, shuffled
uniformly at random and divided 80/10/10, stratified by class, as a standard
image-classification pipeline produces when samples are partitioned without
regard to source capture. Because windows are cut from continuous
captures, window $b$ and window $b{+}1$ (0.3\,s apart, same flows, same hosts,
same burst) are assigned independently, so most test windows have a
near-duplicate neighbour in training.

\textbf{E2: capture level, same attack episode.} Splits are formed at the
granularity of whole source captures, never individual windows. Test captures
are disjoint from training captures, but both are drawn from the \emph{same}
attack execution on \texttt{03-11-2018}. Concretely, of that day's eight
sub-captures, six (1402 attack windows) form the training set and the trailing
two (165 attack windows) form this held-out set.

\textbf{E3: episode level, cross-day.} An entire attack day is held out: the
three \texttt{01-12-2018} sub-captures (966 attack windows) form the test set,
and no capture from that day appears in training, so the model is evaluated on
an attack execution it has never partially observed.

The design of E2 is what makes this study more than a set of loosely comparable
numbers. E2 and E3 are evaluated \emph{from the same trained checkpoint}, with
the same encoding, labels and capture-blocking mechanism, differing in exactly
one respect: whether the held-out captures come from the attack day the model
trained on. That single-factor difference licenses a causal reading and carries
the paper's central claim. E1 additionally differs in encoding, so E1\,$\to$\,E2 is
reported as a bound rather than a clean decomposition; the resulting coverage
is made explicit in the caption of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}.

E3 additionally uses a cluster-validated labeling variant as a robustness
measure: $k$-means ($k=2$) over per-window flow features, accepted as an attack
split only if the selected cluster's mean SYN ratio exceeds the other cluster's
(a flood-signature gate), falling back to schedule labels otherwise. E2 uses
the identical labels, so this does not confound E2\,$\to$\,E3.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

Results follow the order the investigation proceeded: reproduce the headline
numbers this method family is known for, tighten the separation until something
breaks, then interrogate the break.

\subsection{Setup} Offline evaluations ran on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~3070 with MindSpore~2.6.0,
mindcv~0.3.0~\cite{mindcv}, Python~3.10.12 and CUDA~11.6, seed~42. All numbers
come from scripted, re-runnable evaluations released with the code.

\subsection{Same-Episode Baseline: E1}
\label{sec:e1}
Under E1 the system reproduces the high same-episode performance reported for
this method family: 98.10\% test accuracy on 1845 windows, macro F1 0.9375, and
DDoS recall of exactly 1.000 on both validation and test (133/0 and 135/0),
every error being a benign window flagged as an attack (33 and 35
respectively).

Two controls confirm this is not an artifact of imbalance or evaluation
bookkeeping: an always-benign classifier scores 92.7\% accuracy but macro F1
only 0.481, and a label-blind balanced subsample of 270 test images (135 per
class, seed 42) classifies 267 correctly.

At this point the system looks close to solved. The rest of this section is an
attempt to find out whether it is.

\subsection{Tightening the Separation: the Ladder} \label{sec:ladder}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} is the paper's central result: the same system evaluated
at each of the three levels defined in Section~\ref{sec:protocols}.

\textbf{The controlled result: crossing to an unseen attack episode is
catastrophic.} From one checkpoint, held-out same-episode captures give DDoS F1
0.866 while a held-out attack day gives 0.419, a drop of 0.447 with model,
encoding, labels and blocking mechanism all fixed. Overall accuracy falls from
0.9948 to 0.7805. Decomposing E3, precision is what collapses (0.278) while
recall largely holds (0.851): the model still finds most attack windows but
floods the operator with false alarms on traffic it has not seen before. After
the label correction of Section~\ref{sec:audit} these become 0.307 and 0.863.

The consequence is stronger than it first appears. Because E2 is
\emph{already} capture-blocked, capture-level blocking alone does not secure
cross-episode generalization: a test set can be perfectly disjoint at capture
level and still be easy, belonging to the same attack execution with the same
tooling, victim and rate profile. This is the packet-image analogue of the
constraints argued for in~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}.

\textbf{The suggestive result: capture-level blocking within an episode appears
to cost little.} Enforcing that no test window shares a capture with any
training window moves DDoS F1 from 0.885 to 0.866. No conclusion is drawn that
window-level leakage is unimportant, because E1 and E2 also differ in encoding.
The observation bounds the combined effect of the two changes as small, but
cannot attribute that smallness to either one: a genuine window-blocking
penalty offset by an encoding gain would produce exactly these numbers.

The configurations that can be defended are therefore reported, and the
byte-dump runs at E2 and E3 are left unmeasured rather than interpolated.

\subsection{Is the Collapse a Labeling Artifact?} \label{sec:audit} A large
drop invites an obvious alternative explanation: perhaps E3 is not measuring
generalization at all, but the schedule-plus-SYN-count rule used here
mislabeling the held-out day. Before any conclusion is drawn from
Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, that explanation was tested directly.

The false positives are concentrated, which makes the question tractable: 97\%
of the 2\,136 E3 false positives fall in the two sub-captures containing that
day's labeled attack windows, while the other 450 stems show a 1.68\% rate. The 2\,071 flagged windows in those captures were cross-referenced against
CICFlowMeter's official per-flow \texttt{Label} column ($\pm 2$\,s matching),
evidence independent of both the derived labels and the model's predictions. Of
these, 86 (4.2\%) match a \texttt{Syn} flow and are therefore confirmed
labeling errors, 0 match a \texttt{BENIGN}-only flow and so are confirmed model
errors, and 1\,985 (95.8\%) fall outside the export's coverage and are
unverifiable.

Relabeling only the 86 confirmed cases raises E3 DDoS F1 from 0.419 to 0.453.
For the 1\,985 unverifiable windows, feature statistics show them busier than
confirmed true negatives in the same capture (mean SYN ratio 0.080 versus
0.044; mean packet count 302.6 versus 122.4), but visual inspection shows
sparse background traffic rather than the banded flood signature of confirmed
attack windows.

The scope of what this establishes is stated deliberately. It confirms a 4\%
label-boundary error rate among flagged windows with zero confirmed benign
flows flagged; correcting those errors moves DDoS F1 by 0.034, leaving the great
majority of the 0.447 gap unexplained by labeling. It does \emph{not} establish
that the remaining 96\% are correctly labeled: they are unverifiable, not
verified. The feature and visual evidence is consistent with most being genuine
false positives but is weaker than official labels, so the claim is bounded
accordingly. This is weaker than excluding the two captures outright would
permit, implying DDoS F1 $\approx 0.93$, but it is what the evidence
supports.

\subsection{Can Unsupervised Detection Sidestep the Problem?} \label{sec:unsup}
If labeling is a persistent worry, an appealing escape is to stop using labels
altogether. That was tested as well. Two anomaly detectors were trained on
benign windows only, never exposing a DDoS label, under E3.

A convolutional autoencoder scoring by reconstruction error reached test ROC
AUC 0.601, but validation ROC AUC was 0.288, materially \emph{below} chance. A
below-chance detector is usually a bug; here it is a property of the data. DDoS
windows have a nonzero-pixel fraction of 0.0159 against 0.0589 for benign
windows, i.e.\ $3.7\times$ \emph{sparser}: a flood is a torrent of
near-identical minimal segments concentrating mass into few bins, whereas
ordinary traffic scatters widely. In this representation the
extreme sparsity and concentration of SYN-flood windows make them comparatively
easy to reconstruct, violating the expected ordering in which attack samples
receive higher reconstruction or distance scores: a well-fitted model scores
attacks as \emph{more} normal than normal traffic.

Deep SVDD~\cite{ruff2018deep} shows similarly weak separation, consistent with
the same representation-level difficulty. After arresting a textbook
hypersphere collapse with a minimum-embedding-variance floor, it reached ROC
AUC 0.519, near chance, and its encoder is autoencoder-pretrained, so it
inherits the same representation property.

These are reported as negative results with an identified cause rather than
tuning failures. The practical implication is that anomaly-detection framings
of DDoS should verify that their representation actually places attack traffic
on the high-score side before relying on that ordering.

\subsection{Representation Transfer} A final control confirms the two encodings
are not interchangeable, which is why the E1 row of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} is
not treated as substitutable for a byte-dump run at E2. A window-trained
checkpoint applied to per-packet images (19\,300 samples) yields macro F1
0.497, argmax never once predicting DDoS, so the 98.8\% headline accuracy is
entirely the benign majority. Symmetrically, a per-packet checkpoint on window
images (1\,845 samples) gives macro F1 0.473 and zero DDoS recall. Deployment
must construct images exactly as training did, a constraint easy to violate
silently, since both configurations produce plausible-looking accuracy.

% ============================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Gap Means} Neither the E1 number nor the E3 number is
claimed to be wrong or final; they answer different questions. E1 and E2 ask
whether a model can recognize the rest of a partially observed attack episode,
and the answer is nearly perfectly. E3 asks whether it can recognize an episode
never observed, and there it retains recall 0.851 at precision 0.278.

For a deployed detector E3 is the relevant question, and precision 0.278 means
roughly three false alerts per true one: workable behind consecutive-window
voting or threshold tuning, unusable as a direct alarm. Reporting only E1 or E2
would present a system that appears to need no such mitigation.

This mirrors, at finer granularity, what Cantone et
al.~\cite{cantone2024crossdataset} report across datasets: the problem is not
confined to transfer between separately collected corpora, but appears between
two attack executions inside a single dataset. The recommendation that follows
is narrow. Papers in this family should state split granularity explicitly and,
where a dataset contains more than one attack execution, report a cross-episode
result alongside any same-episode one, at the cost of one extra evaluation on
data already collected. Because E2 is itself capture-blocked and still scores
0.866, this rests on the controlled E2\,$\to$\,E3 comparison alone.

\subsection{Deployment} The same lesson has a concrete operational form. The detector runs on a 2-vCPU
Huawei ECS instance: \texttt{tcpdump} writes rotating 0.3\,s captures, each is
encoded and classified, and one JSON record per window is logged with its DDoS
probability. The service sustains continuous operation on CPU alone without backlog,
establishing feasibility; per-window latency is not instrumented, so no
throughput claim is made (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Deployments that threshold
the score rather than taking argmax should note that under E1 probabilities are
moderately miscalibrated (expected calibration error $\approx 0.074$) with an
F1-maximizing threshold near 0.87 rather than 0.5~\cite{guo2017calibration},
and that a threshold tuned on same-episode data has no guarantee of
transferring.

Two caveats follow. Internet background traffic differs substantially from
laboratory capture conditions, a larger shift than E3 measures, so logged
probabilities represent classifier suspicion, not confirmed incidents. And as a
live instance of the finding reported here, the deployed path builds per-packet
images while the checkpoint was trained on window images, exactly the mismatch
shown above to collapse DDoS recall to zero.

\subsection{Limitations} \label{sec:limits}

The controlled result is E2\,$\to$\,E3; E1 also changes encoding, so it bounds
rather than isolates window-level blocking. A single seed and only two
SYN-flood executions make the 0.447 gap a point estimate from one held-out
episode. Augmentation is inherited from the natural-image recipe: under FlowPic
the horizontal axis is time, so \texttt{RandomHorizontalFlip} reverses temporal
order and \texttt{RandomResizedCrop} rescales bins; both are applied identically
in every condition and cannot explain the gap, but a no-flip ablation would
settle it. One architecture is evaluated, with no claim that it beats SYN-rate,
flow-feature or neural baselines, and latency and adversarial robustness are
not assessed.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SYNShield, a packet-image SYN-flood detector on Huawei MindSpore, was built to
measure how much of this method family's reported performance survives
increasing train/test separation. Holding checkpoint, encoding and labels
fixed, DDoS F1 falls from 0.866 on held-out captures from the training episode
to 0.419 on a held-out attack day; an audit against official per-flow labels
confirms a 4\% label-boundary error rate and raises it only to 0.453. A random
window split gives 0.885, close to the same-episode blocked result, though that
comparison is confounded by an encoding change. Two unsupervised baselines fail
because attack windows here are sparser, not denser, than benign ones.

The conclusion is specific: packet-image DDoS detection is a promising
direction whose measured performance depends strongly on whether the test set
contains an attack execution the model has already seen, and capture-level
blocking alone does not establish that it does not. Future work should complete
the encoding $\times$ separation grid, report multi-seed variance, add
classical baselines, and extend to datasets with more than two attack
executions.
